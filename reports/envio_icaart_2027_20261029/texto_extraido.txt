A Qwen–CLIPS Neuro-Symbolic Architecture for Plan Generation in
Service Robotics: Design, Implementation, and Comparative Evaluation
with Direct GPT

Keywords:

Service Robotics, Neuro-Symbolic Planning, Large Language Models, CLIPS, QLoRA, RoboCup@Home,
GPSR.

Abstract:

Transforming natural-language commands into robot plans requires distinguishing semantic interpretation,
planning, and executor compatibility. We present Qwen–CLIPS with Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B interpreters fine-tuned using QLoRA on 40,000 examples, compared with direct GPT using structured output. The
exploratory offline evaluation, bounded by the implemented coverage of the General Purpose Service Robot
(GPSR) task in the RoboCup@Home league and contrasting cases, comprises 60 English commands. For the
46 commands within the local generator’s scope, symbolic fulfillment was 220/230, 215/230, and 216/230
runs for 3B, 7B, and GPT, respectively; scenario exceptions are kept separate. Qwen retained its goals and
plans across runs, including failures. GPT exhibited literal plan changes for 24/60 commands and changes in
an operational-sequence projection for 9/60; these differences do not automatically imply errors. Incompatible
arguments, omissions, and redundant steps were identified despite structural validity. The results characterize
stability, coverage, and observable resources on a development set without measuring physical execution.

INTRODUCTION

Service robots must transform human instructions
into actions compatible with their navigation, perception, manipulation, and interaction modules. This requires interpreting goals, entities, and relations and
producing a plan that respects the executor’s repertoire and parameters. Language models provide linguistic flexibility (Vaswani et al., 2017; Shi et al.,
2023); explicit rules support inspection of symbolic
expansion (NASA Johnson Space Center, 1991; Giarratano and Riley, 2005).
We focus on General Purpose Service Robot
(GPSR), a task in RoboCup@Home, a competition
for service robots in domestic environments. GPSR
presents spoken commands combining tasks such as
finding people or objects, transporting objects, and interacting with users. We delimit coverage through the
families, structures, and vocabulary of a locally extended generator and the actions available in the service robot. This scope does not represent all possible GPSR commands or universal robotic planning.
We evaluate fulfillment within the intended coverage,
linguistic variation, and requests exceeding its boundaries.
We compare Qwen–CLIPS, with fine-tuned
Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B interpreters, against
direct GPT. The former separates neural interpretation from rule-based expansion; the latter generates

goals and a plan in one response. We ask how these
configurations differ in fulfillment, consistency, linguistic robustness, and observable cost for the specified tasks. Our initial hypothesis anticipates greater
structural validity, traceability, and consistency from
Qwen–CLIPS and greater flexibility from GPT for
novel formulations; we examine these dimensions
separately.
We contribute an intermediate goal representation,
its connection to CLIPS and a service-robot executor,
two QLoRA-adapted interpreters, and a reproducible
comparison of three configurations on 60 commands
with five runs per system. Evaluation ends at the symbolic plan and retains failures, clarifications, and rejections.

RELATED WORK

CLIPS represents knowledge through facts and
forward-chaining production rules (NASA Johnson
Space Center, 1991; Giarratano and Riley, 2005). Its
behavior depends on an explicit rule and fact base,
which supports inspection while delimiting coverage.
Language models can relate instructions to symbolic
representations or actions (Shi et al., 2023); constraining their output format through a schema does not itself establish semantic correctness.
Neuro-symbolic architectures combine neural

learning with explicit knowledge representation
(d’Avila Garcez et al., 2015; Besold et al., 2017).
LLM+P (Liu et al., 2023) translates a description
into PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language),
a language for specifying planning domains and problems, and uses a classical planner to search for a plan.
Our separation of responsibilities is related, but uses
canonical goals and production-rule expansion rather
than PDDL search. Comparison with direct GPT examines the consequences of this separation under a
shared repertoire.
LoRA adapts low-rank matrices while freezing
original weights (Hu et al., 2022); QLoRA combines adapters and quantization to reduce memory
(Dettmers et al., 2023). These techniques support specializing Qwen2.5 interpreters (Yang et al., 2024) for
a bounded domain and comparing two sizes with a
common corpus.

PROBLEM FORMALIZATION

We represent the transformation as x → G → P, where
x is the command, G = (g1 , . . . , gn ) the goals, and P =
(a1 , . . . , am ) the actions. The canonical vocabulary
comprises go, find, take, deliver, place, guide,
follow, count, tell, save, answer_question,
and greet. For find and count, kind distinguishes
people from objects; qualifiers express attributes such
as gesture, posture, or clothing.
Goals retain precedence, reference, and state dependencies. For example, taking an object precedes
delivering it, and navigating to a room establishes the
spatial context for a search. We distinguish syntactic
and structural validity, semantic fidelity of G, fulfillment by P, and physical success; the last is outside
the measurement scope.

Fine-Tuning Corpus

We construct command–goal pairs through shared
context between linguistic and semantic generation,
preserving entities and dependencies across subtasks. A family groups related task structures;
a template specifies a way of expressing them
with variable entities. Both models use the same

passed the CLIPS expansion check. This is a prefix
check rather than a random corpus sample.

5.2

Inference and Symbolic Contract

We normalize Qwen inputs through lowercasing,
whitespace, punctuation, politeness expressions, and
aliases. We load the quantized model and adapter and
use do_sample=False, repetition penalty 1.05, and
at most 128 new tokens. The adapter preserves step,
goal, entity, type, destination, relation, and qualifiers.
CLIPS processes goals in order and updates location
and held object as expected effects rather than physical observations. Figure 5 illustrates an expansion.
For GPT we use responses.create, model
identifier gpt-5.6-terra, a customized prompt,
version 1.6, and at most 8,192 output tokens. The
strict schema requires status, message, goals,
plan, start_note, and end_note. The validator
checks structure, status coherence, numbering, parameter count, and closure; semantic compatibility
with the executor is assessed separately. ROS 2 integration connects the semantic node, adapter, and
CLIPS node; campaigns use independent programs
that record the plan without sending it to controllers.

6.3

Evaluation Criteria

We use symbolic fulfillment as the primary criterion: plans must preserve subtasks and constraints,
use executor-compatible actions and arguments, and
respect necessary dependencies. Valid JSON does not
guarantee satisfaction of the command.
Fulfills indicates task satisfaction; fails indicates
an observable omission, incompatibility, or violation.
Not verifiable indicates insufficient evidence to resolve an identity or protocol dependency; such cases
would remain in the denominator of evaluable tasks,
separate from successes and confirmed failures. No
cases remain in this category after clarifying the scenario conditions. Not applicable means the case
specification does not require an executable plan: in
blocks A/F/X, we separately assess the adequacy of
clarification or rejection. We do not assign this judgment merely because the system fails or produces no
plan. An observable defect determines failure even
when other effects cannot be verified.

6.4

Scenario Conditions and Review
Procedure

In named-recipient tasks, the only person in the room
represents the requested person. For following tasks,
the first person found at the specified location is the
intended recipient, and instruct_follow_to_* activates the executor special case that carries out following and its additional steps. These conditions do
not extend to counting or GPSR in general and do not
imply physical verification. Interaction and memory

protocols retain the scope documented in the review.
We transfer previous judgments only to identical evidence. For the 70 GPT runs differing from
R1 (42 variants across 28 commands), we use AIbased review against requirements and executor code,
kept separate from our explicit acceptance decisions.
Subsequent confirmation of the following conditions
is recorded separately and does not extend to the
remaining automated judgments. We consider responses without plans equivalent when they retain an
adequate request for information or explanation of a
limitation or contradiction. Each record retains evidence, judgment provenance, and exceptions.

DISCUSSION

7.1

Interpretation of the Results

The larger interpreter did not yield greater fulfillment:
3B achieved more correct runs than 7B in the main
block, whereas 7B showed lower latency and higher
GPU memory use. These observations describe the
measured configurations rather than a causal relationship between size, quality, and speed. The Qwen–
CLIPS separation helps locate whether a constraint is
lost in goals, adaptation, or expansion.
Repeatability must be interpreted alongside fulfillment. Without sampling, Qwen retains both successes
and failures; repeating an incorrect output does not
make it more reliable for the task. GPT exhibits wording and sequence variation, including corrections and
new omissions. Its clarification and rejection behavior is adequate in the diagnostic cases but changes for
the attribute conjunction. We therefore assess decision stability and coverage alongside literal equality.
Incompatible arguments show that JSON structure
and parameter count do not guarantee a usable plan.
Verification must cover admitted values, references,

and action effects. Our initial hypothesis receives descriptive support for Qwen consistency, but these data
do not establish its general superiority in structural
validity. Traceability is a property of decomposition
and records rather than a measured comparative rate;
linguistic robustness is limited to the included formulations.

7.2

Scope of the Evidence

Fine-tuning data were split by rows rather than by
family or template, and the experimental set informed
protocol development. Results do not establish generalization to unseen families. Five repetitions and
paraphrases of an intent remain related; we present
descriptive statistics without treating them as 900 independent samples. Small diagnostic blocks, retrospective classification, and AI-based review delimit the strength of the conclusions. Simultaneous
changes in model, adaptation, and planner prevent attributing all differences to CLIPS.
For deployment, Qwen–CLIPS offers control of
weights, rules, and versions and local inference without external connectivity once installed, at the cost
of hardware, energy, and maintenance. GPT externalizes part of that infrastructure through an API requiring connectivity and usage expenditure. Selection
depends on coverage and operational conditions; this
evaluation does not calculate comparative total ownership cost.

7.3

Future Work

The next evaluation will use independent commands
with predefined blocks and criteria. We propose
extending argument validation, addressing filtered
counting and weight-based selection, exploring clarification in Qwen–CLIPS, and testing plans through
physical execution. Additional families and languages will support studying transfer beyond current
coverage.
